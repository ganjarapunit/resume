The Shallows and the Depths: IELTS Reading, C2

1Reading you, warm up

Speak aloud, 2 to 3 sentences each. Then type one line to lock it in.

  1. Do you skim more or study more in English?
  2. When did you last get lost in a text and forget time?
  3. What kind of IELTS passage slows you down most?
Model: I tend to skim news and messages to filter, but I study work reports closely. I lost track of time with a long feature on cities. Dense scientific passages with long noun phrases slow me down most.

2Predict from the title

THE SHALLOWS AND THE DEPTHS: Reclaiming Attention in an Age of Skimming

Speak your predictions first. What could shallows and depths mean? Story, argument, or research summary? What three ideas do you expect?

Model: I expect an argument about superficial online reading versus deep focused reading, probably with brain research and practical advice for learners. Shallows suggests skimming, depths suggests deep reading.
IELTS tip: 30 seconds on the title and first lines saves minutes later.

3Skim A and B, 2 minutes

A. We read more than ever, and understand less than we dare admit. Messages, headlines, captions. The average smartphone user meets more words in a day than a 17th-century scholar met in a lifetime. Yet most of that meeting is glancing, not reading. We skim to cope, and skimming, repeated often enough, becomes our default.

B. Neuroscientists make a useful distinction. Skimming is efficient for spotting relevance. Deep reading is slower and more embodied. It draws on regions linked to empathy, inference and memory. When you linger over a complex sentence, your brain treats it almost like lived experience. When you skim it, you keep the gist, but lose the texture.

Set a 2 minute timer. No dictionary. Then answer.

What is the main idea of A and B?

4Say the gist in 30 seconds

Cover the text. Speak for 30 seconds starting with: This part is mainly about... or The writer is saying that...

Model: This part is mainly about how we skim to handle overload, and how that turns automatic. The writer is saying that skimming is useful for finding relevance, but deep reading gives you memory and feeling for a text.

5Meet C and D, headings match

C. This matters beyond literature. In one study, students who read a story with full attention were later better at reading ambiguous social situations than peers who skimmed summaries. Deep readers held several perspectives at once. Skimmers remembered facts, but missed motive.

D. Schools feel this daily. Exams like IELTS still reward what skimming erodes: following an argument, spotting paraphrase, telling what is stated from what is only implied. Readers who hunt only keywords fall for traps, because writers rephrase ideas and hide qualifications in small clauses.

Headings: i. A skill tests still demand. ii. What deep readers gain. iii. A habit shaped by overload.

7Close read E, words in context

E. The response is not to abandon technology, but to train attention as a skill. Slow-reading groups, print sabbaths, single-tab reading. All share one principle: decide in advance what deserves depth. Preview to select, then commit fully. In IELTS terms, skim to orient, scan to locate, then read closely to decide. Fluency, in the end, is knowing which gear to use.

Type short glosses. One or two words are enough.

8Paraphrase lab

Say each in your own words aloud. Change the grammar, not just one word. Then compare.

  1. Skimming, repeated often enough, becomes our default.
  2. You keep the gist, but lose the texture.
  3. Preview to select, then commit fully.
1. If you skim all the time, it turns into your automatic habit.
2. You get the general idea, but miss the nuance and feeling.
3. Look over the text to choose, then give it your full focus.

10Read between the lines

Speak two sentences for each, then reveal and compare.

  1. Why might a strong speaker still struggle with IELTS reading?
  2. Why do test writers rephrase instead of repeating words?
  3. What could texture mean in your own reading?
1. Because conversational fluency does not guarantee patience for long arguments. If you hunt only keywords, you miss qualifications.
2. To test meaning, not word matching. Paraphrase separates skimmers from readers.
3. Texture could be tone, irony, hesitation, the human layer behind facts.

11Voice and stance

How does the writer feel overall?
Evidence: understand less than we dare admit and fall for traps show concern. Train attention as a skill and knowing which gear to use show agency and hope.

1260 second summary

Plan 30 seconds, speak 60 seconds. Cover all four: problem, brain science, school link, solution. Record on your phone if you can.

Model, about 60 seconds: We see so much text that skimming becomes automatic. Brain science says skimming spots relevance, but deep reading builds empathy and memory. That matters for school, because IELTS rewards following arguments and spotting paraphrase, which skimmers miss. The fix is to train attention. Preview to choose, then read what matters closely.

13Argue it

Statement: Skimming is not decline. It is a smart adaptation to too much information. Do you agree? Speak with I partly agree or disagree because, plus one example from work or studies.

Frame: I partly agree because filtering is essential, you cannot read everything closely. That said, adaptation becomes decline when we lose the ability to switch gears. In my work, I skim reports to triage, then block time for the two that need depth.
