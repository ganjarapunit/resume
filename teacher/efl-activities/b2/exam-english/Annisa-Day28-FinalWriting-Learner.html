IELTS model, 250 words

Some universities treat safety as a separate topic near the end of an engineering degree. I strongly disagree. Safety should be a core thread in every engineering programme because engineers create systems that affect people, equipment, and the environment. First, safety gives students a reason to question technical data. During a 30-day heat-exchanger audit at Pertamina RU IV, I learned that a number is not only a result. It can be a warning. If students connect this habit to their maths, chemistry, and design work, they will understand why accurate data matters and why assumptions must be checked. Second, early safety teaching can improve professional judgement. A K3 safety certificate is useful, but a list of rules is not enough. Students should practise identifying hazards, explaining risk, and proposing safer design changes. These skills help them communicate with engineers and supervisors later in their careers. Some people may argue that safety reduces time available for technical design. This concern has some validity, since engineering degrees already cover a wide range of subjects. However, safety does not need to be a separate long course. It can be taught through lab decisions, case studies, design reviews, and equipment problems. In this approach, students practise technical content while learning to consider people and consequences. Therefore, universities should make safety a core part of every engineering degree. The goal is not to produce more rules to memorise. It is to train engineers who see risk, ask questions, and design systems responsibly from the beginning.

Report model, 209 words

Recommendations for Process Engineering Training Manager. Purpose: This report recommends a practical reporting session for process engineers. Evidence base: a 30-day heat-exchanger fouling audit at Pertamina RU IV, a K3 safety certificate, later training with more than 90 students, and mentoring of 10 teams. The audit showed that strong technical work also needs clear communication. Data from a 30-day period had to be explained to senior engineers, with uncertainty and safety implications made visible. Training students to connect evidence to a decision can therefore improve communication and confidence in technical teams. The recommended session has three parts. First, learners will identify the purpose and named reader. Second, they will separate measurements from assumptions and connect each finding to relevant safety or process evidence. Third, they will write one recommendation that states the action, responsible role, and timing. I recommend one 60-minute workshop followed by one reviewed sample report. This format is short enough for a busy engineering team, but it gives learners a complete writing cycle: purpose, evidence, judgement, and action. The K3 certificate can support safety questions, while the audit and mentoring experience provide authentic process context. The training manager should pilot the session with one group, collect the strongest report, and revise unclear instructions before wider use.

Blog model, 275 words

Engineering students spend months solving technical problems, but many leave university with little practice explaining those problems to other people. I believe process engineering programmes should include more authentic technical writing. A report is not an exercise placed after the design work. It is part of the design work. During a 30-day heat-exchanger fouling audit at Pertamina RU IV, I had to turn operating readings into a short report for senior engineers. The calculations mattered, but the reader also needed to know the purpose, the limits of the data, the safety implications, and the recommended next step. This experience changed how I saw writing. Clear language helped me check my own reasoning. If an explanation is weak, the underlying thinking may also be weak. Writing also helps engineers work with others. As a lab assistant, I later trained more than 90 students, and mentoring 10 teams showed me how many decisions depend on clear instructions and shared evidence. Strong technical work has little value if another engineer cannot act on it. One concern is time. Adding separate writing lessons could reduce design or laboratory practice. That risk is real, but the answer is not to ignore communication. Writing can be taught through the existing work: one report section, one design explanation, one peer review, and one revision. These micro-tasks use the same evidence the students already produce. Process engineering education should not treat writing as an optional skill. Engineers create systems that affect people, equipment, and the environment. They need to explain those systems with enough clarity for others to act safely. Teach technical writing throughout the degree, and the whole engineering team becomes stronger.
