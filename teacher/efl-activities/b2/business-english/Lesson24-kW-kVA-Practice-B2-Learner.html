The conversation

Listen again as many times as you need. Use the player controls to pause and replay.

Show the full conversation

Alex: Good morning, Mr. Keller. Thank you for calling back. Did you have a chance to look at the comparison sheet I sent?

Mr. Keller: Good morning, Alex. Yes, I did, and that is actually why I am calling. There is something I do not understand. Your quotation says 500 kVA and 400 kW. Competitor X simply says 500 kW. Are you offering a smaller machine?

Alex: That is a very good question, and a common one. No, the machine is the same size. The two numbers describe two different things. Let me explain them in simple words, one step at a time.

Mr. Keller: All right, go ahead.

Alex: First, kW. kW is the real power the machine uses. It is the power that does the work. For your site, that is 400 kW.

Mr. Keller: So 400 kW is the useful power.

Alex: Exactly. Second, kVA. kVA is the apparent power, including losses. It is always equal to or higher than kW. In our unit it is 500 kVA.

Mr. Keller: So the gap between 400 and 500, that is the losses?

Alex: Yes, that is exactly right. Losses are the part of the power you pay for and do not use.

Mr. Keller: And Competitor X says 500 kW. That would mean no losses at all?

Alex: That is the point I want to make carefully. A power factor of 1.0 means no losses. Very few real machines reach 1.0, most are around 0.8. If a competitor advertises 500 kW, it is worth asking whether they mean kW or kVA, and what their power factor is.

Mr. Keller: I see. So the number on its own is not enough.

Alex: Correct. That is why I always give three numbers together: kW, kVA and power factor. Third, power factor is the ratio of kW to kVA. For our unit, 400 divided by 500 is 0.8.

Mr. Keller: Okay. And why should I care about that ratio?

Alex: Because it tells you how efficiently the machine uses electricity. A low power factor means more losses and higher electricity costs. A high power factor means lower costs. In simple words, the better the power factor, the less you pay for power you never use.

Mr. Keller: That makes sense. We did talk about stable output voltage under load as well, didn't we?

Alex: We did, and that comes from the voltage regulator we discussed last time. It keeps the output voltage stable even when the load changes. That is a separate point from the rating, but it is part of the same story about quality.

Mr. Keller: Right. Now I understand why you always write the numbers in that order.

Alex: Exactly. We start with kW, the real power that does the work. Then we add kVA, the total including losses. Then we show the power factor, the ratio between them. Then we name the losses, the part you pay for and do not use. And we end with efficiency, which is the whole point.

Mr. Keller: Our engineer wrote in his note that it uses 500 kW stuff. I had to read it twice.

Alex: I am glad you mentioned that. In professional English we would say: The unit consumes 500 kW of real power. We say consumes, not uses, and we name the unit and the type of power. Small changes, but they sound much more professional.

Mr. Keller: Understood. Consumes, real power. I can use that.

Alex: Good. So if you remember one thing from this call, remember this: always compare the three numbers together, not just the headline figure. A high power factor means lower costs.

Mr. Keller: That is clear. Thank you, Alex. Could you add the power factor to the comparison sheet, so I can show my team?

Alex: Of course. I will send the updated sheet today. Does that work for you?

Mr. Keller: Yes, today is fine. Thank you. Goodbye.

Alex: Thank you, Mr. Keller. Goodbye.
